# Translation Guidelines

## Phase 1: Initial Translation

Translate the Korean source into clear, natural English written for a middle to high school reading level.

### File Handling — Write Before Proceeding
At the end of Phase 1, write the translation to the output file immediately. Phase 2 begins by reading that file fresh — not from memory, not from what was just written in the session. The file is the source of truth. Treat Phase 2 as a different person picking up where Phase 1 left off.

### Preserve Formatting & Narrative Style
Keep the original layout including paragraph breaks and pacing. Match the flow and rhythm of the original writing as closely as possible.

### Cultural Localization with Context
Balance Korean cultural nuance with English readability. Localize idioms and phrases where appropriate. If a cultural element cannot be easily localized:
- Keep the original term or a close equivalent in the sentence
- Add a translator note on its own line below the sentence

Format: *(T/N: ...)*

### Convey Core Sentiment
Preserving the emotional tone and underlying sentiment is the top priority. The feeling behind each line — whether subtle, dramatic, comedic, or introspective — must be clearly reflected in the English version.

### Smooth and Natural English
Use simple, clear grammar and phrasing so the English reads like a polished novel for middle to high school readers. Keep sentences easy to follow. Avoid complex vocabulary, overly formal language, and long or complicated sentence structures. Use casual or local phrasing if it fits the tone.

### Punctuation — No Long Dashes or Colons
Long dashes (—) and colons (:) are not used. Not mid-sentence, not for pauses, not for emphasis, not for interrupted speech, not anywhere. If a sentence wants a dash or colon, restructure it. Break it into two sentences, reorder the clauses, or find a word that carries the weight the dash was doing. Never reach for the dash as a rhythm tool — solve the rhythm problem instead.

### Tense
All narration should be in past tense. Dialogue may retain present tense if that reflects how the character would naturally speak.

## Phase 2: Localization & Rewrite

Read the Phase 1 output file. Compare it against the Korean original. The goal is not just translation but a version that reads as if it were originally written for an English-speaking audience, while preserving the emotional core of the Korean.

### File Handling — Write Before Proceeding
At the end of Phase 2, overwrite the output file with the revised draft. Phase 3 begins by reading that file fresh. Do not carry forward memory of what was written — open the file and work from what is actually there.

### Cultural Localization with Context
Replace literal Korean slang, humor, or informal language with natural English equivalents that feel authentic to the story's world and tone. Keep uniquely Korean terms only if they carry cultural weight — explain them using the T/N format, not as academic footnotes. Adjust cultural references so they land for readers unfamiliar with Korean culture while keeping the emotional impact intact.

### Preserve Core Sentiment & Emotional Tone
Prioritize the feeling behind each scene. If the Korean repeats for effect, find a natural English rhythm that delivers the same intensity without feeling redundant. For narrator voice specifically, refer to the POV & Narrative Style section of novel_info.md before rewriting any introspective or reflective passage.

### Smooth, Natural English Flow
Write in a style that feels like a polished novel for middle to high school readers. Use punchy, clear sentences. Favor casual, vivid phrasing over literal translation. Dialogue should sound authentic to the character and the world they inhabit.

In ensemble and group scenes, keep dialogue moving. Attribution and reaction beats should be woven into the flow rather than stacked as separate paragraphs around each line. The goal is for the reader to feel the energy of the room, not watch it from a distance.

### Sentence Integration & Rhythm
Korean prose frequently separates ideas into short, stacked sentences for emphasis or pacing. In English, this can read as choppy rather than punchy. During Phase 2, actively consider whether consecutive short sentences should be merged into a single fluid sentence — particularly in narration and internal monologue. The test: would an English novelist write these as one sentence? If yes, merge them. Preserve the separation only when the gap between sentences is doing emotional work — a pause, a pivot, a landing. When in doubt, read it aloud. Choppy loses; flowing wins.

Example. When the Korean separates context-setting information across several short sentences — establishing who is involved, what the situation is, why it matters — these can usually be merged into one or two fluid sentences in English. The reader gets the same information without being made to stop three times for a single idea. The merged version reads as narration; the separated version reads as a list.

### Standalone Beats
Internal monologue, emotional pivots, and thematically significant observations often need to stand alone — even if the Korean syntax could support folding them into a surrounding sentence. If a line is the *point* of the passage, give it space. Don't bury the weight in the middle of a longer sentence. This applies especially to regression callbacks, character self-awareness moments, and tonal pivots.

Example. A literal read might produce: "This was the first time doing X since the regression, so it felt different, and I was nervous." That buries the weight. The correct handling isolates the beat:
> *First time since the regression. I couldn't help feeling the weight of it.*
The beat lands because it has room to land.

### Descriptive Passages — Texture Over Punch
The "punchy, clear sentences" principle applies to action, dialogue, reaction, and emotional beats. It does not apply equally to descriptive passages: a character's appearance, a location being established, a performance or creative work being described. In these passages, favor connected, flowing sentences that let the reader settle into the scene. Fragments and staccato rhythm pull the reader out of immersion at exactly the wrong moment. The test: is the reader meant to *feel* something building, or meant to *react* to something landing? Building calls for flow. Landing calls for punch.

Example. A location being established for the first time should draw the reader in — connected sentences, sensory detail accumulating. Fragments and punchy cuts work against that. Reserve the short, sharp sentence for the moment something *happens* inside that location, not for the description of the location itself. The same applies to a character's appearance: let the image build across a sentence or two before landing the payoff.

### Naturalization — Ask the Right Questions
For each line or exchange, ask:
- What is the emotional register of this moment — and does my English match it?
- What would an English writer reach for here, rather than what does the Korean say?
- Is there a more vivid, specific, or idiomatic word choice available?
- Does the dialogue sound like this character, or does it sound like translated dialogue?
- Would a native English reader trip on any word, phrase, or construction here?

### Preserving the Quality of the Writing
The quality of the writing must survive translation, not just the meaning. If a line is poetic in Korean, find the English that feels poetic — pay attention to rhythm, word choice, and shape. If a line is a gut punch, make sure it hits in English. If a moment is tender, the English should carry that weight without deflating it. Ask not just "is this accurate?" but "does this line do what the original line does?"

For every significant line — chapter opens, chapter closes, emotional peaks, internal monologue breakthroughs — stop and ask: does this hit? If the answer is uncertain, rewrite it until it does. These lines should never be left on the first attempt.

### Tense
All narration in past tense. Dialogue remains natural to the character.

### Informal & Conversational Language
Convert informal language, internet slang, or expressive shorthand into natural English equivalents that preserve the tone and energy of the original. Specific mappings for recurring expressions should be recorded in `cultural_phrases.md`.

## Phase 3: Editing Pass — Source Comparison

Read the Phase 2 output file. Do not work from memory of what was written — open the file and read what is actually there. This phase is not a vibe check. It is an active, structured comparison against the Korean source, conducted on the live file. Its purpose is to catch three categories of failure that Phase 2 rewrites can introduce.

This pass must be completed in full before sign-off. It is not optional and cannot be skipped or merged with Phase 2.

### 1. Meaning Fidelity
Read each sentence of the Phase 2 draft alongside the corresponding Korean source line. Verify that nothing has been omitted, compressed, or flattened.

What to catch:
- Specific details present in the Korean that did not survive into Phase 2 (setting, context, named subjects, scope)
- Sentences where the emotional intent was preserved but factual content was dropped
- Closing lines, chapter titles, and thematically significant lines — these must be checked with particular care, as rewrites tend to prioritize rhythm over completeness

Failure model: A closing line rendered as "The meta breaker had arrived" when the Korean specifies the streaming world as the industry disrupted, names the act of disruption explicitly, and frames the moment as a public announcement of arrival. The Phase 2 version kept the energy. It lost the meaning.

### 2. Error Introduction
Check the Phase 2 draft for anything that was added during the rewrite that is not present in the source.

What to catch:
- Phrasing, details, or implications that drifted in during localization
- Interpretive additions that go beyond what the Korean says — even if they feel natural
- Tone shifts in narration or dialogue that change characterization

When in doubt, return to the Phase 1 draft and confirm what the source actually said before deciding whether the Phase 2 change is justified.

### 3. Formatting — Dash Audit
Read through the Phase 2 output file line by line. For every line containing a long dash (—), copy that line here explicitly. There are no permitted uses. Every instance is a violation and must be rewritten before proceeding. After all fixes are applied, read the file once more to confirm none remain.

## Final Review — Mandatory Checks

After Phase 3, conduct a final read of the complete chapter for overall consistency, tone, and guideline adherence.

### Consistency
- All character names, titles, and address forms match the character bible
- All terminology matches established translations in terminology.md
- Cultural phrases handled consistently with cultural_phrases.md

### Tone
- Narration voice matches the novel's established tone (dry, introspective, lightly self-deprecating)
- Dialogue feels authentic to each character's established speech pattern
- No section drifts toward melodrama, over-explanation, or unearned sentiment

### Formatting
- Paragraph breaks and pacing match the source structure
- Internal monologue formatting (italics) applied consistently
- Kill count formatting consistent throughout action sequences

## Status-jockeying (기 싸움)
- Korean term: 기 싸움 (氣 싸움)
- Literal meaning: Energy/spirit battle; aura contest
- Social function: Informal, wordless jockeying for dominance and status
  between cast/crew, especially early in a production — establishing
  hierarchy through attitude, body language, and small confrontations before
  any explicit conflict happens. Recognized and named analytically by
  characters (not always experienced as hostile in the moment).
- Narrative intent: Frames a scene's tension as structural (an industry
  mechanic everyone recognizes) rather than personal, and lets a character
  "win" by refusing to register the attempt rather than by counter-attacking.
- Rendering strategies: Translating the abstract label alone ("status fight,"
  "status game") under-delivers — it names the category without showing the
  reader what's actually happening. Show the concrete tactics (a stare held
  too long, a pointed compliment, a refusal to defer) so the dynamic is
  legible from the behavior itself, not the label.
- Example transformations: [none recorded yet — flag chapter/line when found]
- First appearance: 68 (see also `cultural_phrases.md`'s 기 싸움 entry for the
  lexical gloss of the term itself)
- Notes: Ch. 68's read-through arc (Choo Mi-yeon vs. Hee-yeon) is the
  clearest example so far — the confrontation is explicitly framed by Kang as
  기 싸움 to Hee-yeon beforehand, and resolves because Hee-yeon doesn't
  register it as a fight at all.

## Direct eye contact as confrontation
- Korean term: [none — behavioral, not lexical]
- Literal meaning: n/a
- Social function: Sustained, direct eye contact (especially from a junior
  to a senior, or in a tense social moment) can read as a challenge or
  breach of deference in Korean social context, more strongly than the
  equivalent behavior does in American convention.
- Narrative intent: Used to signal defiance, confidence, or a power
  imbalance being tested without a word being said.
- Rendering strategies: A literal rendering ("he looked her straight in the
  eye") reads as neutral or even positive (confidence, honesty) to an
  American reader, losing the confrontational charge. Make the charge
  explicit through a reaction beat or interior narration ("she didn't look
  away, and that was the problem") rather than the raw action alone.

- Notes: Watch for this specifically in scenes with an explicit

## Appearance-based teasing read as affectionate
- Korean term: [none — behavioral, not lexical]
- Literal meaning: n/a
- Social function: Teasing a friend/love interest by commenting on their
  body or appearance is common as an affectionate register in Korean
  interpersonal dynamics; American teasing between the same relationship
  types leans on personality/behavior rather than physical commentary, so
  the same move can misread as crude or objectifying if translated straight.
- Narrative intent: Signals closeness/comfort between characters without
  romantic weight (or with deliberately ambiguous romantic weight).
- Rendering strategies: Consider shifting the target of the tease from body
  to personality/behavior when the physical-commentary version would read
  as ungenerous or ill-fitting for the character's voice in English, while
  preserving the affectionate function of the line.
- Example transformations: general craft note from 2026-07-26 planning
  discussion — not yet tied to a specific chapter/line; flag one when found.
- First appearance: [not yet tied to a chapter]
- Notes: Judge case by case — some body-based teasing reads fine in English
  when it's clearly self-deprecating or mutual; the miss is specifically
  when it reads as one-sided commentary on someone's appearance.
